Quick answer: To perform a technical SEO audit, crawl the whole site, compare what you find with Google Search Console's indexing and Core Web Vitals reports, check robots.txt, sitemaps, canonicals, redirects, speed, mobile usability and structured data, then rank every issue by how much it affects indexing and traffic.

What a Technical SEO Audit Checks

A technical SEO audit looks at the parts of a website that decide whether search engines can find, read and trust its pages. It ignores what the page says and focuses on how the site is built: status codes, crawl rules, canonical tags, page speed, mobile layout and structured data.

The goal is not a long list of warnings. It is a short, ranked list of the problems that are actually costing you visibility, with a clear owner for each fix.

  1. DiscoverCan Google find every important URL?
  2. CrawlIs it allowed to fetch them?
  3. RenderDoes the content load for bots?
  4. IndexIs each page eligible and canonical?
  5. ExperienceIs it fast and mobile friendly?

The 9-Step Technical SEO Audit

  1. 01

    Crawl the Whole Site

    Run a crawler from the homepage and record every URL, its status code, canonical, title, meta robots tag and the links pointing to it. Note the total number of HTML pages; you will compare it with Google's numbers next.

  2. 02

    Compare With Google's Index

    Open the Page Indexing report in Search Console. If your crawl found 120 pages but Google indexes 40, the gap is your first lead. Look at the reasons Google gives for each excluded URL.

  3. 03

    Check robots.txt

    Confirm robots.txt is not blocking pages, CSS or JavaScript you want indexed, and that it lists your sitemap. A single misplaced Disallow line can hide a whole section.

  4. 04

    Review the XML Sitemap

    The sitemap should list only live, indexable, canonical URLs that return a 200 status. Redirects, 404s and noindexed pages in a sitemap send Google mixed signals.

  5. 05

    Audit Canonicals and Duplicates

    Each page should point its canonical tag at itself unless it is a deliberate duplicate. Check HTTP and HTTPS, www and non-www, trailing slashes and URL parameters.

  6. 06

    Fix Redirects and Broken Links

    Find internal links that hit 4xx errors or go through redirect chains, and point them straight at the final URL. Googlebot follows up to 10 redirect hops, but every hop wastes time.

  7. 07

    Test Speed and Core Web Vitals

    Run PageSpeed Insights on each main template: home, service, blog post, contact. Google's "good" thresholds are LCP of 2.5 seconds or less, INP of 200 ms or less and CLS of 0.1 or less.

  8. 08

    Check Mobile Usability

    Google indexes the mobile version of your site. Make sure content, links and structured data on mobile match desktop, and that text and buttons are usable on a phone.

  9. 09

    Validate Structured Data

    Test key pages in the Rich Results Test. Fix errors first, then add missing types such as Organization, LocalBusiness, Service or Article where they describe the page honestly.

How to Prioritize What You Find

Most crawls produce dozens of warnings. Sort them by one question: how much does this stop important pages from being indexed, ranked or used? Fix anything that blocks indexing first, then anything that affects many pages, then the rest.

IssuePriorityWhy
Important pages with noindex or blocked by robots.txtHighThey cannot appear in search at all
Canonicals pointing to the wrong URLHighGoogle ranks a different page than you intended
Mixed content or pages still on HTTPHighSecurity warnings and split signals
Internal links to 404 pagesMediumWasted crawling and a poor visitor experience
Slow LCP on key templatesMediumVisitors leave before the page loads
Missing schema on service pagesMediumSearch engines get less explicit information
Long titles or duplicate meta descriptionsLowMostly affects how results look, not eligibility

A Real Example From a 345-URL Audit

For a smart home and AV integrator, a full crawl covered all 345 URLs and produced 40 findings: 4 issues, 17 warnings and 19 opportunities. Ranked by impact, the top of the list looked like this:

74URLs with mixed content
39Canonicalized URLs
38URLs still on HTTP
8Internal 4xx errors

None of those were visible to the business owner, and all of them were fixable. On a different integrator's site, the same type of work ended with a crawl showing zero issues and PageSpeed scores of 97 on mobile and 100 on desktop.

Audit After Every Big Change

Run a quick crawl after a redesign, a platform migration, a new plugin or a bulk content update. Most serious technical problems arrive with changes like these.

Technical SEO Audit Checklist

  • Crawl completed and page count compared with Search Console
  • No important page carries noindex or is blocked by robots.txt
  • Sitemap lists only 200-status, indexable, canonical URLs
  • One version of the site: HTTPS, one hostname, consistent slashes
  • No internal links to 4xx pages or redirect chains
  • Core Web Vitals pass on the main templates
  • Mobile content matches desktop
  • Structured data validates without errors

Questions Answered

How Often Should I Do a Technical SEO Audit?

A full audit once or twice a year suits most small business sites, with a quick crawl after any redesign, migration or major plugin change.

Can I Do a Technical SEO Audit Myself?

Yes, for a small site. The free tools above cover the essentials. The hard part is deciding what matters, which is where an experienced auditor saves time.

What Is the Difference Between a Technical SEO Audit and a Full SEO Audit?

A technical audit covers crawling, indexing, speed and site structure. A full SEO audit adds content, keywords, competitors and backlinks.

Do I Need Search Console for an Audit?

It is the single most useful source, because it shows how Google itself sees your site. Set it up first if you haven't.
